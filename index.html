      How it works

      An antipode is the point at the other end of a line drawn through the centre of the Earth: flip the sign of the latitude and shift the longitude by 180°. Madrid (40.4° N, 3.7° W) maps to 40.4° S, 176.3° E, on New Zealand’s North Island.

      The upside-down world layer draws every continent mirrored through the Earth’s centre. Wherever the pink outlines fall on land, that land has land on the other side.

      Using this data, only about 13% of the world’s land has land at its antipode, roughly 4% of the planet’s surface. Almost everything else is opposite ocean, most often the Pacific.

      Country borders come from Natural Earth at 1:50m scale (via world-atlas). Distance to land is measured to the nearest coastline point and is accurate to a few kilometres; sea names are approximate. Populations are rounded metro-area figures.
